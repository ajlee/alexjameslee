Seven years - but my PhD thesis called "Power, inequity, and socio-ecological transformation in UK rewilding" is done.

I started off my PhD optimistic about rewilding as a solution for environmental collapse. However, the data I collected indicated that rewilding is being reshaped to suit the requirements of profit and power. As such, I argue the dominant forms of rewilding are failing to meaningfully address the causes of the biodiversity crisis.

In the UK, rewilding tends to be focused on encouraging the biggest landowners to rewild, that is, about 36,000 people who own about 50% of rural land. As such, rewilding appears to be concentrating environmental authority among elite actors. The structure of the ELMS subsidies, that direct most money to large landowners, appears likely to reinforce this.

The data I collected also showed that rewilding provides elites with increased social status. The media often present elite rewilders as saviours, and an aristocratic, Knepp-based approach as the ideal form of rewilding. This provides large landowners with increasing authority on environmental issues, which thereby allows them more influence over public perceptions, what solutions are seen as sensible, and what policies the government puts forward. Additionally, my research suggested a level of ignorance among elites regarding biodiversity. They generally are not experts in this area, but despite this lack of knowledge, they tend to call the shots, simply because they have status, land and money.

Elites are also frequently framed as philanthropic - motivated by "doing good". However, most openly admit they started rewilding for financial reasons. As a result, ecological choices over rewilding actions such as reintroductions may be focused on what is profitable rather than what is best for nonhuman life. The reliance on elite actors also reinforces their control over land, for example, justifying use of government funding to fence out the general public and turn their land into exclusive spaces which the public has to pay to enter, and positioning public access as undesirable and damaging.

My thesis went onto argue that environmental actions that reproduce capitalism and enormous class inequities cannot address environmental breakdown. These are at odds with the continued existence of life on this planet. However, I also explored rewilding as a means of challenging capitalism, and found fragments of a more radical vision and practice of rewilding that could be part of a more equitable future, through pushing back against private property, through everyday rewilding, and through democratic debate.
